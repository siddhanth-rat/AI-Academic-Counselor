USA Student Visa

## Overview
Generally, foreign nationals who wish to travel to the United States must first obtain a visa, either a nonimmigrant visa for a temporary stay, or an immigrant visa for permanent residence. You must have a student visa to travel to the United States to study. Your course of study and the type of school you plan to attend determine whether you need an F visa or an M visa.

To enter the United States to attend:	You need the following visa category:
University or college	F
High School
Private elementary school
Seminary
Conservatory
Another academic institution, including a language training program
Vocational or other recognized nonacademic institution, other than a language training program	M

Students cannot travel on the Visa Waiver Program or with Visitor Visas
You must have a student visa (F or M visa) to travel to the United States to study. You may not study after entering on a visitor (B) visa, unless you are eligible for and have obtained a change of status from USCIS, or through the Visa Waiver Program (VWP), except to undertake recreational (non-credit) study as part of a tourist visit. For more information on the VWP, see Visa Waiver Program.

For short periods of recreational study, a Visitor (B) visa may be appropriate
A visitor (B) visa permits enrollment in a short recreational course of study, which is not for credit toward a degree or academic certificate. Learn more about Visitor Visas.

Study leading to a U.S. conferred degree or certificate is not permitted on a visitor (B) visa, even if it is for a short duration. For example, a student in a distance learning program that  requires a period of time on the institution’s U.S. campus must obtain a student (F or M) visa prior to entering the United States.

## Student Acceptance at a SEVP Approved School
The first step to studying in the United States is apply to a SEVP-approved school in the United States.  If the SEVP-approved school accepts your enrollment, you will be registered for the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) and must pay the SEVIS I-901 fee.  The SEVP-approved school will issue you a Form I-20.  After you receive the Form I-20 and register in SEVIS, you may apply at a U.S. Embassy or Consulate for a student (F or M) visa.  You must present the Form I-20 to the consular officer when you attend your visa interview.

If your spouse and/or children will live with you in the United States while you study, they must also enroll in SEVIS, obtain individual Form I-20s from the SEVP-approved school, and apply for a visa (but they do not pay the SEVIS fee).

Visit the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) Student and Exchange Visitor Program (SEVP) website to learn more about SEVIS and the SEVIS I-901 Fee.

Visit the Department of State EducationUSA website to learn about educational opportunities for undergraduate and graduate study, and an overview of the application process.  You can also visit the DHS Study in the States school search page to search for SEVP-certified schools.

## How To Apply
There are several steps to apply for a visa. The order of these steps and how you complete them may vary by U.S. Embassy or Consulate. Please consult the instructions on the embassy or consulate website. 

Complete the Online Visa Application
Online Nonimmigrant Visa Application, Form DS-160 – Learn more about completing the DS-160. You must: 1) complete the online visa application, and 2) print the application form confirmation page to bring to your interview.
Photo –You will upload your photo while completing the online Form DS-160. Your photo must meet the Photograph Requirements.
Schedule an Interview
Interviews are generally required for visa applicants with certain limited exceptions. Consular officers may require an interview of any visa applicant.

You should schedule an appointment for your visa interview at the U.S. Embassy or Consulate in the country where you live. You may schedule your interview at another U.S. Embassy or Consulate, but be aware that it may be more difficult to demostrate that you qualify for a visa outside of the country where you live. 

Wait times for interview appointments vary by location, season, and visa category, so you should apply for your visa early. Review the interview wait time for the location where you will apply:

New Students – Student (F and M) visas for new students can be issued up to 365 days before the start date of a course of study.  However, you will not be allowed to enter the United States on your student visa more than 30 days before the start date.

Continuing Students - Student (F and M) visas for continuing students may be issued at any time, as long as the student is currently enrolled at a SEVP-approved school or institution and in SEVIS.  Continuing students may enter the United States at any time before classes start.

## Prepare for Your Interview
Fees - You must pay two separate fees. First, the SEVIS I-901 fee, which is currently $350 for F-1 and M-1 students. Second, the non-refundable DS-160 visa application fee, which is currently $185. You must pay these before your interview. If your visa is approved, you may also pay a visa issuance fee, if applicable to your nationality. 




## Gather Required Documentation
Gather and prepare the following required documents before your visa interview:

Passport valid for travel to the United States – Your passport must be valid for at least six months beyond your period of stay in the United States (unless exempt by country-specific agreements). Each individual who needs a visa must submit a separate application, including any family members listed in your passport.
Nonimmigrant Visa Application, Form DS-160 confirmation page.
Application fee payment receipt, if you are required to pay before your interview.
Photo – You will upload your photo while completing the online Form DS-160. If the photo upload fails, you must bring one printed photo in the format explained in the Photograph Requirements. 
Certificate of Eligibility for Nonimmigrant (F-1) Student Status-For Academic and Language Students, Form I-20 or Certificate of Eligibility for Nonimmigrant (M-1) Student Status for Vocational Students, Form I-20 – Your school will send you a Form I-20 once they have entered your information in the SEVIS database. You and your school official must sign the Form I-20.  All students must be registered in the Student and Exchange Visitor System (SEVIS). Your spouse and/or minor children, if they intend live in the United States with you, will each receive an individual Form I-20.
## Additional Documentation May Be Required
A consular officer will interview you to determine your qualifications for a student visa, and may request additional documents, such as evidence of:

Your academic preparation, such as:
Transcripts, diplomas, degrees, or certificates from schools you attended; and
Standardized test scores required by your U.S. school;
Your intent to depart the United States upon completion of the course of study; and
How you will pay all educational, living and travel costs. (CRITICAL: You must provide bank statements showing liquid funds that cover at least the total amount for one full academic year of tuition and living expenses, exactly as the amount is listed on your Form I-20). 
Review the instructions for how to apply for a visa on the website of the U.S. Embassy or Consulate where you will apply.

## Attend Your Visa Interview
A consular officer will interview you to determine whether you are qualified to receive a student visa. You must establish that you meet the requirements under U.S. law to receive a visa.

Ink-free, digital fingerprint scans are taken as part of the application process. They are usually taken during your interview, but this varies based on location.

After your visa interview, the consular officer may determine that your application requires further administrative processing.  The consular officer will inform you if this is required.

After the visa is approved, you may need to pay a visa issuance fee (if applicable to your nationality), and make arrangements for the return of the passport and visa to you.  Review the visa processing times to learn more.

## Entering the United States
A visa does not guarantee entry into the United States.  A visa only allows a foreign citizen to travel to a U.S. port-of-entry (generally an airport) and request permission to enter the United States. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS), U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) officials at the port-of-entry have authority to permit or deny admission to the United States.

After you present your passport, visa, and Form I-20 at the port-of-entry, a CBP official will make this decision.  Upon admission, the CBP official will provide an admission stamp or paper Form I-94, Arrival/Departure Record.

Learn about procedures for students (with F or M visas) entering the United States on the CBP website under Arrival Procedures for Students or Exchange Visitors.  Learn about admissions and entry requirements, restrictions about bringing food, agricultural products, and other restricted/prohibited goods, and more by reviewing the CBP website.

## Extending Your Stay
Foreign students in the United States with F visas must depart the United States within 60 days after the program end date listed on Form I-20, including any authorized practical training.

Foreign students may request an extension through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) website (see the USCIS Extend Your Stay page). Additional information to maintain student status is on the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement SEVP website under Maintaining Your Immigration Status While a Student or Exchange Visitor.

Failure to depart the United States on time will result in being out of status. Under U.S. law, visas of individuals who are out of status are automatically voided (Section 222(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act).  Any multiple entry visa that was voided due to being out of status will not be valid for future entries into the United States. 

Failure to depart the United States on time may also result in you being ineligible for visas in the future in certain cases. Review Visa Denials and Ineligibilities and Waivers: Laws to learn more.

## Change of Status
If your plans change while in the United States (for example, you marry a U.S. citizen or receive an offer of employment), you may be able to request a change in your nonimmigrant status to another category through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS). See Change My Nonimmigrant Status on the USCIS website to learn more.

While you are in the United States, receiving a change of status from USCIS does not require you to apply for a new visa.  However, once you depart the United States you must apply for a new visa at a U.S. Embassy or Consulate in the appropriate category for your travel.

## Additional Information
There is no guarantee you will be issued a visa. Do not make final travel plans or buy tickets until you have a visa.
For information about working in the United States during your study:
- On-Campus Work: F-1 students may work on-campus up to 20 hours per week while school is in session, and full-time (40 hours) during official school breaks.
- Off-Campus Work: Strictly prohibited during your first academic year. After one year, it is only permitted via authorized Curricular Practical Training (CPT) or Optional Practical Training (OPT), or in cases of severe economic hardship.
- Post-Graduation (OPT): Upon graduating, F-1 students can apply for 12 months of Optional Practical Training (OPT) to work in the US in their field of study. STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Math) degree holders can apply for an additional 24-month extension (36 months total).
If you have a temporary break in your study, view the information on the SEVP website under Do Students Returning from Temporary Absences Need New Visas? If your student visa is still valid, but you are outside the United States, you should consult with your Designated School Officials. 
## Spouse and children
Your spouse and unmarried, minor children who intend to reside with you during your study may apply for F-2 or M-2 visas. Although SEVIS fee payment is not required, your school must issue them an individual Form I-20, which is required to apply for their visas. You must provide a copy of your F-1 or M-1 visa and provide proof of relationship. (CRITICAL WARNING: Dependents on an F-2 visa are strictly prohibited from working or engaging in any employment while in the United States).
Your minor children are permitted to attend school in the United States while accompanying you.
U.S. Embassies and Consulates adjudicate visa applications for same-sex spouses in the same way that we adjudicate applications for opposite sex spouses.
A valid U.S. visa in an expired passport is still valid. Unless canceled or revoked, a visa is valid until its expiration date. If you have a valid visa in your expired passport, do not remove it from your expired passport. You may use your valid visa in your expired passport along with a new valid passport for travel and admission to the United States.



Can I enter the United States more than 30 days in advance?
Students on F or M visas are not permitted to enter the United States earlier than 30 days before the start date of their program.  If you wish to enter earlier than 30 days before your start date, you must separately apply and qualify for a visitor (B) visa.  

After you are admitted to the United States by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) officials in visitor (B) visa status, you must separately apply to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) for a change of status to student (For M) status prior to the start of your program.  You may not begin your course of study until the change of status is approved, and you may encounter lengthy processing times.  You may also depart the United States and re-enter on your student (F or M) visa.

## Maintaining Your F-1 Student Status (Critical Rules)
Maintaining valid F-1 status is your personal responsibility. Violating any of these rules can result in losing your status, being deported, and being banned from re-entering the US.

- Full-Time Enrollment Requirement: You MUST be enrolled full-time every semester (except your final semester). Full-time is defined by your school, but is typically at least 12 credit hours for undergraduates and 9 credit hours for graduates. Dropping below full-time without prior authorization from your Designated School Official (DSO) will immediately put you out of status.
- Online Course Limit: F-1 students are only allowed to count ONE online or distance-learning course (up to 3 credit hours) towards their full-time requirement per semester. You cannot be enrolled in a fully online program on an F-1 visa while in the United States.
- Address Reporting: You must report any change of address to your DSO within 10 days of moving.
- Program End Date: You must complete your degree before or by the program end date listed on your Form I-20. If you need more time, you must request a program extension from your DSO BEFORE the current end date expires.
- Annual Re-enrollment: You must re-enroll and maintain student status every semester. Taking an unauthorized semester off (a "leave of absence") without your DSO's approval will cause you to lose your F-1 status.

## Duration of Status (D/S)
Unlike most other visas, the F-1 visa does not have a specific expiration date for your stay. Instead, you are admitted for "Duration of Status" (D/S), meaning you can stay in the US as long as you are maintaining your full-time student status and making normal progress toward completing your degree.

Your visa stamp itself (in your passport) can expire while you are in the US without a problem — you can remain in the US legally. However, if you travel abroad and need to re-enter the US, you will need a valid (unexpired) F-1 visa stamp to re-enter.

## Grace Periods
- After Program Completion: After your program end date on your I-20, you have a 60-day grace period to either depart the US, apply for a program extension, transfer to another school, or change your visa status. You may NOT work during this grace period.
- After OPT Ends: After your OPT authorization ends, you have a 60-day grace period to depart, change status, or begin a new program.
- After Program Withdrawal/Termination: If you withdraw or are expelled from your program, you only have a 15-day grace period to depart the US.

## Curricular Practical Training (CPT)
CPT allows F-1 students to work off-campus in a job directly related to their field of study as part of their curriculum (e.g., a required internship).

- Eligibility: You must have been in valid F-1 status for at least one full academic year before being eligible for CPT.
- Authorization: CPT must be authorized by your DSO and entered into SEVIS BEFORE you begin working. Working without prior authorization is a serious status violation.
- Part-time vs. Full-time CPT: If you use 12 months or more of full-time CPT, you become permanently ineligible for OPT. Part-time CPT (20 hours or fewer per week) does not affect your OPT eligibility, regardless of duration.
- CPT is employer and job-specific: Your authorization is tied to a specific employer and role. You cannot use one CPT authorization to work for a different employer.

## Optional Practical Training (OPT)
OPT allows F-1 students to gain work experience in their field of study either before graduation (Pre-Completion OPT) or after graduation (Post-Completion OPT). Most students use Post-Completion OPT.

- Standard OPT Duration: 12 months.
- STEM OPT Extension: Students with a degree in a STEM field (Science, Technology, Engineering, or Mathematics) from a US institution may apply for a 24-month extension, for a total of 36 months of OPT.
- Application Timing: You must apply for OPT through USCIS no more than 90 days before your program end date and no later than 60 days after your program end date. Processing can take 3-5 months, so applying early is critical.
- Unemployment Limits: During standard OPT, you cannot be unemployed for more than 90 days in total. During the 24-month STEM extension, you cannot be unemployed for more than 150 days (cumulative, including days used during standard OPT). Exceeding the unemployment limit terminates your OPT and ends your legal stay.
- Work Authorization Card: You must receive your Employment Authorization Document (EAD card) from USCIS before you can legally begin working during OPT.
- STEM OPT Employer Requirements: For the STEM extension, your employer must be enrolled in the E-Verify program. You cannot do self-employed or volunteer work during OPT and claim it counts toward your unemployment days.

## Travel While on F-1 Visa
- Valid Visa Stamp Required for Re-entry: If your F-1 visa stamp in your passport has expired and you travel outside the US (except brief trips to Canada or Mexico), you MUST obtain a new F-1 visa stamp at a US Embassy or Consulate abroad before you can re-enter the US.
- Travel During OPT: You can travel internationally during OPT. To re-enter, you need a valid passport, a valid F-1 visa stamp, your EAD card, and a letter from your employer confirming your job offer/employment.
- Travel During CPT: You can travel internationally during CPT but need your valid visa stamp and your I-20 with the CPT authorization to re-enter.
- Automatic Visa Revalidation: For brief trips ONLY to Canada or Mexico (less than 30 days), your expired F-1 visa can be automatically revalidated for re-entry, as long as you don't apply for a new visa while abroad. This does NOT apply to citizens of countries on the State Sponsor of Terrorism list.

## Health Insurance
Health insurance is NOT mandated by the federal government for F-1 students. However, the vast majority of US universities require students to have health insurance and automatically enroll them in the university's own health insurance plan, charging the premium to the student's account. Students can typically waive this if they provide proof of comparable private coverage. Medical costs in the US are extremely high, so not having insurance can be financially devastating.

## Social Security Number (SSN)
F-1 students are NOT eligible for a Social Security Number (SSN) unless they have authorization to work (i.e., on-campus employment authorization, CPT, or OPT). Once you have work authorization, you can apply for an SSN at your local Social Security Administration (SSA) office.

## Transferring Schools
You can transfer from one SEVP-approved school to another. You must notify your current DSO of your intention to transfer. Your current school will release your SEVIS record to your new school. You must enroll at the new school by the transfer release date specified in SEVIS, otherwise you will fall out of status. You cannot work off-campus between the transfer release date and beginning enrollment at the new school.

## Changing Your Degree Level or Major
You can change your major or degree level (e.g., from a Bachelor's to a Master's program) at your same school. You must inform your DSO, who will update your SEVIS record and issue you a new Form I-20 reflecting the change.

## Visa Interview Tips and Common Rejection Reasons
The most common reason for F-1 visa denial is failure to demonstrate "non-immigrant intent" under Section 214(b) of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The consular officer must be convinced that you intend to return to your home country after your studies.

Strong evidence to bring to demonstrate ties to your home country includes:
- Proof of family ties (family members remaining in home country).
- Proof of property ownership or a lease in your home country.
- A letter from a future employer or proof of a job waiting for you at home.
- Proof of financial assets in your home country.
- A clear and convincing statement of your study plan and career goals in your home country.

Other common rejection reasons:
- Inability to clearly explain your study plan or why you chose that specific US school.
- Finances that appear insufficient or whose source cannot be explained.
- Previous US visa violations or immigration law violations.
- Criminal history.

## Driving in the United States
An F-1 visa is NOT a driver's license. To drive legally in the US, you must obtain a state-issued driver's license from the state where you live. Rules vary by state, but you will typically need your passport, your I-20, your I-94 record, and proof of your US address to apply. International driving permits are only valid for a very short period in most states.

## Taxes for F-1 Students
F-1 students are NOT exempt from all taxes — this is a very common misconception. Here is what you need to know:

- Tax Filing Obligation: You are required by US law to file a federal tax return every year, even if you earned no income. If you had no US-sourced income, you must file Form 8843. If you did have income, you must file Form 1040-NR in addition to Form 8843.
- The 5-Year Rule (Non-Resident Alien Status): For the first 5 calendar years in the US on an F-1 visa, you are considered a "Non-Resident Alien" (NRA) for tax purposes. This means you pay taxes only on US-sourced income and are NOT subject to the "Substantial Presence Test." After 5 years, your tax status may change to Resident Alien.
- FICA Taxes (Social Security & Medicare): During your first 5 years as an NRA, you are EXEMPT from Social Security and Medicare tax (FICA) on wages earned from on-campus employment or authorized CPT/OPT. If your employer incorrectly withholds FICA taxes, you must request a refund from your employer or the IRS.
- Tax Treaties: The US has tax treaties with many countries that may reduce your tax rate or exempt certain types of income. Check whether your home country has a tax treaty with the US.
- Tax Deadline: The US federal tax deadline is typically April 15th each year for the previous tax year.
- Non-Resident Aliens cannot file jointly with a US citizen/resident spouse using the standard resident tax forms.

## Cap-Gap Extension (Bridging OPT to H-1B)
If you are on Post-Completion OPT and your employer files an H-1B petition on your behalf by April 1st (for the lottery), and your OPT would expire before the H-1B start date of October 1st, you are automatically granted a "cap-gap" extension. This extends your OPT work authorization and F-1 status to bridge the gap until your H-1B status begins on October 1st. If your H-1B petition is rejected in the lottery, your cap-gap extension ends and you must depart within your 60-day grace period.

## Reinstatement of F-1 Status
If you fall out of F-1 status (e.g., dropped below full-time enrollment without authorization), you have two main options:

- Depart and Re-enter: Leave the US and re-enter on your F-1 visa. This is often the simplest option.
- Apply for Reinstatement: File Form I-539 with USCIS to request reinstatement of your F-1 status. USCIS will only approve this if the violation was not your fault OR if you can demonstrate that denial would cause severe hardship. CRITICAL: You cannot work during the reinstatement application period, and you may be denied even after waiting many months. Processing times can be 6-12+ months.

## Reduced Course Load (RCL) Authorization
There are limited circumstances where an F-1 student may be authorized to take fewer than the required full-time credits. You MUST get written authorization from your DSO BEFORE dropping below full-time. Valid reasons include:

- Academic difficulty in your first semester at a new school (one-time only).
- A documented medical or mental health condition certified by a licensed medical doctor, clinical psychologist, or licensed clinical social worker (maximum 12 months total across your entire program).
- Your final semester where fewer courses are needed to complete your degree requirements.

Taking a reduced course load for any other reason, or without DSO authorization, is an immediate status violation.

## Authorized Early Withdrawal / Medical Leave
If you need to take a medical leave of absence, you must work with your DSO. Your DSO will terminate your SEVIS record for an authorized early withdrawal. You will have a 15-day grace period to depart the US. When you are medically cleared to return, your school must create a new SEVIS record and issue you a new I-20, and you must re-enter the US on your F-1 visa.

## Summer and Holiday Break Rules
- Between Academic Years (Summer): You are NOT required to be enrolled full-time during the summer if you were enrolled full-time the previous spring semester AND plan to enroll full-time the following fall semester. You may work on-campus full-time during this period.
- Other Official Breaks: You can also work full-time on-campus during any other official school break periods (e.g., winter break).
- You must maintain a US address and valid SEVIS record even during breaks.

## Banking and Finances
- Opening a Bank Account: Most major US banks require a Social Security Number (SSN) or an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) to open an account. However, some banks and credit unions (e.g., Bank of America, Citibank) allow international students to open an account with just their passport, I-20, and I-94. Look for banks with specific "international student" account programs.
- ITIN: If you have no SSN and need to file taxes, you can apply for an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN) using IRS Form W-7.
- Sending Money Abroad: There are no restrictions on an F-1 student sending money back to their home country, but large transfers may be reported by banks to the IRS.

## English Language Requirements
There is no federal government English language requirement for an F-1 visa. However, every accredited US university or college will have its own English proficiency requirements for admission. The most commonly accepted tests and typical minimum scores are:
- TOEFL iBT: Typically 80-100+ for most universities (top programs may require 100+).
- IELTS Academic: Typically 6.5 overall for most universities.
- Duolingo English Test: Increasingly accepted, typically 110-120+.
- Some universities have conditional admission programs where you can start at an English language institute on the same campus before transitioning to your degree program.

## Academic Probation and Its Effect on Status
If you are placed on academic probation by your university, it does not automatically affect your F-1 status. However, if probation leads to academic dismissal (expulsion), your SEVIS record will be terminated by your DSO and your F-1 status will end. You will then have only 15 days to depart the US.

## Applying for a Green Card (Permanent Residence) from F-1 Status
An F-1 visa is a non-immigrant visa. Applying for a Green Card (permanent residence / immigrant visa) while on an F-1 visa is technically possible, but it is extremely risky because it signals "immigrant intent," which directly contradicts the core requirement of the F-1 visa (that you intend to return home). 

A consular officer can deny your F-1 visa renewal or your re-entry to the US if they believe you intend to immigrate. The standard path for most F-1 graduates who want to stay in the US permanently is: F-1 → OPT → H-1B work visa (employer-sponsored) → Green Card.

## J-1 Exchange Visitor Visa vs. F-1 Student Visa
Some students may be eligible for a J-1 Exchange Visitor visa instead of an F-1. Key differences:

- J-1 is typically used for specific exchange programs, research scholars, or short-term study.
- J-1 students have "Academic Training" (AT) instead of OPT. AT can last up to the length of the degree program or 18 months (whichever is greater, up to 36 months for post-doctoral training).
- Some J-1 visas have a Two-Year Home Residency Requirement: you must return to your home country for 2 years before applying for an H-1B or Green Card. This is one of the most significant restrictions of the J-1 visa.
- F-1 is generally considered more flexible and better for long-term career goals in the US.

## Unlawful Presence Bars (CRITICAL — Most Severe Consequence)
This is the most severe and permanent consequence of F-1 status violations and is often misunderstood.

If you remain in the US past your authorized period (i.e., after your 60-day grace period expires, or after your SEVIS record is terminated), you begin to accrue "unlawful presence."

- 180+ days of unlawful presence: You will be barred from re-entering the US for 3 years after departure.
- 365+ days (1 year+) of unlawful presence: You will be barred from re-entering the US for 10 years after departure.
- More than 1 year of unlawful presence PLUS an attempt to re-enter illegally: You may be permanently barred from the US.

These bars are triggered the moment you depart the US, making it an invisible trap. A student can unknowingly accrue unlawful presence for months and only discover the bar when they try to re-enter or apply for a new visa.

## Checking Your I-94 Arrival/Departure Record (Critical Step After Every Entry)
Every time you enter the US, CBP creates an electronic I-94 record. This record determines your official authorized period of stay. You MUST check your I-94 record at cbp.dhs.gov/I94 within a few days of every entry into the US to make sure it says "D/S" (Duration of Status) and that all your personal information is correct. If there is an error (e.g., it shows a specific date instead of D/S, or your name is wrong), you must correct it immediately with CBP. An incorrect I-94 can have serious consequences for your status.

## Administrative Processing (221g) — Visa Delays
After your visa interview, a consular officer may issue a "221g refusal," placing your application in "administrative processing." This is NOT a denial; it means your application requires additional review (often a background security check, export control analysis, or verification of credentials).
- Common Triggers: Students studying STEM fields, advanced research, or sensitive technologies (e.g. aerospace, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, quantum computing) listed on the Technology Alert List (TAL) are frequently placed under 221(g) for background verification.
- Timelines: Administrative processing can take anywhere from a few weeks to 60 days, and in some cases, several months. There is no guaranteed or expedited timeline once it begins.
- Action: If you are issued a 221(g), notify your university's International Student Office (DSO) immediately, as it may affect your ability to arrive on time and you may need to defer your admission.
- CRITICAL TRAVEL WARNING: Do NOT purchase non-refundable flight tickets or make permanent travel plans until your visa application has been approved and you have your passport with the physical F-1/M-1 visa stamp in your hand. The embassy and DHS will not take responsibility for flight cancellation fees, and booking tickets does not expedite the processing of a 221(g) hold.

## Selective Service Registration (Male Students Aged 18-25)
Male F-1 students between the ages of 18 and 25 are required by US law to register with the Selective Service System within 30 days of arriving in the US. Failure to register can affect eligibility for future US government benefits, federal jobs, and critically, certain immigration benefits (like naturalization). You can register online at sss.gov. This requirement applies to virtually all male non-citizens residing in the US between those ages.

## State Income Taxes
In addition to federal taxes, most US states also levy their own state income tax on wages earned within that state. Tax rates and rules vary dramatically by state. Some states like Texas, Florida, and Nevada have NO state income tax, while others like California and New York have rates as high as 9-13%. You are typically required to file both a federal return AND a state return for every state where you earned income.

## STEM Designated Degree Program List
Not all degrees that sound like "STEM" qualify for the 24-month STEM OPT extension. The degree must be on the official DHS STEM Designated Degree Program List, which is categorized by specific Classification of Instructional Programs (CIP) codes. Common qualifying fields include: Computer Science, Engineering, Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, Biology, Economics (in some cases), and Data Science. You should verify your specific degree program's CIP code with your DSO before assuming you are eligible for the STEM extension.

## M-1 Visa Specific Rules (Vocational Students)
The M-1 visa is for students at vocational or non-academic institutions. Key differences from F-1:
- M-1 students CANNOT change to a different program or school after arrival.
- M-1 students CANNOT change to F-1 status while inside the US.
- Post-Completion Practical Training for M-1 students is much more limited: you get only 1 month of practical training for every 4 months of study, up to a maximum of 6 months total. There is no STEM extension for M-1 students.
- M-1 students must depart within 30 days of completing their program (vs. 60 days for F-1 students).

## Second Degree on F-1
You can pursue a second Bachelor's degree or a second Master's degree on an F-1 visa. Your school must issue you a new Form I-20 for the new program. You will also be eligible for a fresh 12-month OPT allotment for the new degree level (e.g., if you used OPT for your Bachelor's, you get a new 12 months of OPT eligibility upon completing a Master's degree). However, you cannot do two degrees at the same level at the same time (e.g., two simultaneous Bachelor's programs).

## STEM OPT Reporting Requirements (Form I-983)
The 24-month STEM OPT extension comes with strict, ongoing reporting obligations that are often overlooked. Failure to comply will result in termination of your STEM OPT authorization.

- Training Plan (Form I-983): Before starting the STEM extension, both the student and employer must complete and sign Form I-983, which is a formal Training Plan outlining the learning objectives, skills to be gained, and mentoring structure of the position. This must be submitted to your DSO.
- Bi-Annual Self-Evaluations: Every 6 months throughout the entire 24-month STEM extension, the student must complete a self-evaluation and submit it to their DSO via your school's reporting system.
- Employer Evaluations: The employer must also complete a formal evaluation at the end of the STEM OPT period (and at the 12-month mid-point).
- Job Change During STEM OPT: If you change employers at any point during your STEM OPT, you MUST file a brand new Form I-983 with the new employer and have your DSO update your SEVIS record BEFORE you begin working for the new employer. Working for a new employer without doing this first is a status violation.
- The new employer must also be enrolled in E-Verify.

## School Closure or Loss of SEVP Certification
If your SEVP-certified school unexpectedly closes, loses its accreditation, or has its SEVP certification revoked by ICE, your SEVIS record will be terminated. You will be given a 60-day period to either:

- Transfer your SEVIS record to a new SEVP-approved school and enroll, OR
- Depart the United States.

You do not immediately fall out of status, but if you fail to act within the 60-day window, you will begin accruing unlawful presence. ICE and DHS will typically notify students via email if their school's SEVP status is revoked, which is another reason to always keep your contact information updated with your DSO and school.

## I-20 Travel Signature Requirement (Common Re-Entry Trap)
Every time you travel outside the US and return, your Form I-20 must have a valid travel signature from your DSO. This is one of the most common traps for international students who travel home for holidays.

- Standard Rule: Your DSO's travel signature on page 2 of your I-20 must be dated within the last 12 months.
- During OPT: If you are traveling during your OPT period, the travel signature must be dated within the last 6 months.
- If your signature has expired: You must contact your DSO and get a newly signed I-20 BEFORE you depart the US. A CBP officer can deny your re-entry at the port of entry if your travel signature is expired, even if your visa stamp is valid.
- During STEM OPT: Same 6-month rule applies. You also need your valid EAD card, a valid visa stamp, and a letter from your employer.

Always check your I-20 signature date before booking any international travel.

## Volunteer Work Rules
F-1 students are allowed to do unpaid volunteer work without any USCIS work authorization under specific conditions. However, this is frequently misunderstood and abused, leading to status violations.

- Permitted: Truly voluntary, unpaid work at a qualifying non-profit organization, government agency, or community service organization where you are NOT displacing a paid worker and are not under any obligation or expectation of future paid employment.
- NOT Permitted (Unauthorized Employment): Unpaid internships at for-profit companies are generally considered unauthorized employment by USCIS, regardless of whether you are being paid or not. The fact that you receive no salary does not make it legal — if the position provides a tangible benefit to the company, it may be treated as employment.
- Safe Rule: When in doubt, get CPT authorization from your DSO before starting any internship or work experience at a for-profit company, even if it is unpaid.

## Federal Financial Aid (FAFSA) Ineligibility
F-1 international students are completely ineligible for US federal financial aid. This includes:
- Federal student loans (Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Loans)
- Pell Grants
- Federal Work-Study programs
- Any other aid distributed via the FAFSA (Free Application for Federal Student Aid)

Only US citizens and certain eligible non-citizens (e.g., Green Card holders, refugees, asylees) qualify for FAFSA-based aid. International students must fund their education through:
- Their own personal or family funds (as proven on your Form I-20)
- Institutional scholarships awarded directly by the university
- Home-country government scholarships
- Private international student loans (these are available but typically require a US citizen co-signer)
- External scholarships (e.g., Fulbright, country-specific government programs)

## Business Ownership and Self-Employment on F-1
F-1 students frequently misunderstand what is and is not permitted regarding business ownership.

- Passive Investment: An F-1 student CAN legally be a passive investor or shareholder in a company (including owning equity or stocks) as long as they perform no active work for the business.
- Active Work in Own Business: An F-1 student CANNOT actively work in, manage, operate, or run a business they own without proper work authorization (CPT or OPT). Performing ANY work — including managing employees, attending client meetings, writing code, or making business decisions — counts as unauthorized employment, even if you pay yourself no salary.
- Self-Employment During OPT: During standard or STEM OPT, self-employment is in a legal gray area. USCIS allows it if the student can demonstrate the self-employment is in their field of study and they are the owner of a legitimate business. During STEM OPT, self-employment is explicitly restricted — the student must have a qualifying E-Verify enrolled employer.
- Starting a startup during studies: You may plan, incorporate, and own a startup, but you cannot actively work on it without CPT or OPT authorization.

## Pre-Completion OPT and the Shared 12-Month Pool
Pre-Completion OPT allows F-1 students to work in their field of study before graduating. However, there is a critical rule that most students miss:

- Shared Pool: Pre-Completion OPT and Post-Completion OPT share the same 12-month authorization pool per degree level. Every single month of Pre-Completion OPT you use permanently reduces your Post-Completion OPT by the same amount. For example, if you use 3 months of Pre-Completion OPT, you will only have 9 months of Post-Completion OPT remaining.
- Part-Time During School: Pre-Completion OPT used while school is in session must be part-time (20 hours/week or less). Full-time Pre-Completion OPT is only permitted during official school breaks.
- Application Timing: You must apply for Pre-Completion OPT through USCIS and receive your EAD card before starting work, just like Post-Completion OPT.
- Recommendation: Because of the shared pool, most students are advised to save their full 12 months for Post-Completion OPT rather than using Pre-Completion OPT. If you need work experience during studies, CPT is generally a better option as it does not reduce your OPT eligibility (unless you use 12+ months of full-time CPT).

## Who Is a DSO (Designated School Official)?
A Designated School Official (DSO) is a staff member at your university who has been authorized by the US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) to maintain SEVIS records and provide immigration advising to international students. Every SEVP-approved school must have at least one Primary DSO (PDSO) and may have multiple additional DSOs.

Why this matters:
- Your DSO is the ONLY person who can update your SEVIS record, issue travel signatures on your I-20, authorize CPT/OPT, approve reduced course loads, and process transfers.
- You cannot go directly to USCIS or DHS for most F-1 status actions — they all go through your DSO first.
- Your DSO is typically located in your university's International Student Services Office (ISSO) or International Student Office (ISO).
- You should introduce yourself to your DSO as soon as you arrive, and contact them immediately any time your situation changes (changing majors, financial difficulties, medical issues, travel plans, job offers, etc.).
- Failure to involve your DSO in changes to your status is one of the most common reasons students unknowingly fall out of status.

## Standard OPT Employment Reporting (10-Day Rule)
During ALL OPT (both standard and STEM), you have an ongoing obligation to report employment changes to your DSO. Your DSO then updates your SEVIS record.

You must report the following to your DSO within 10 days of the change:
- Starting a new job (employer name, address, job title, start date, hours per week)
- Losing or leaving a job
- Changing your employer
- Changing your US address or phone number
- Changing your legal name

Failure to report these changes does not immediately invalidate your OPT, but it creates SEVIS discrepancies that can cause serious problems when you apply for visa renewals, H-1B petitions, or re-entry into the US. ICE can also flag your record for non-compliance.

Note: This 10-day reporting obligation is separate from and in addition to the formal bi-annual I-983 reporting requirement that applies specifically to STEM OPT.

## STEM OPT Third-Party Staffing and Consulting Ban
This is one of the most misunderstood and commonly violated rules for STEM OPT students, especially those in the IT consulting and staffing industry.

USCIS has explicitly stated that F-1 students on STEM OPT CANNOT be "staffed out" to a third-party client worksite through an intermediary staffing agency or consulting company. Specifically:
- The E-Verify enrolled employer listed on your Form I-983 must be the entity that actually directs, supervises, and controls your day-to-day work.
- If you work for a staffing company (Company A) but are placed at a client's office (Company B) where Company B controls your work, this violates STEM OPT rules — even if Company A is E-Verify enrolled.
- This arrangement is common in IT staffing and consulting, where many students are placed as contractors at large corporations through a middleman employer. This is not legal under STEM OPT.
- If caught in such an arrangement, your STEM OPT can be revoked and your F-1 status terminated.
- The safe model: You must genuinely work for and be supervised by the E-Verify employer in your day-to-day activities.

## Renewing Your F-1 Visa Stamp Abroad
Your F-1 visa stamp (the sticker in your passport from the US Embassy) can expire while you are legally residing in the US on D/S status. Many students are surprised to discover their visa stamp expired years ago while they were studying — this is perfectly legal and normal, but it becomes a problem when they want to travel internationally and re-enter.

To renew your F-1 visa stamp, you must apply at a US Embassy or Consulate outside the United States. The process is:

1. Complete a new DS-160 visa application form online.
2. Pay the $185 non-refundable MRV visa application fee.
3. Schedule a visa interview appointment at your nearest US Embassy or Consulate (this can be in your home country or any third country).
4. Gather your documents: valid passport, DS-160 confirmation, fee receipt, current valid I-20 (with valid travel signature), SEVIS fee payment receipt from your original enrollment, current university enrollment letter, transcripts, financial proof, and any OPT/CPT authorization if applicable.
5. Attend the interview. The consular officer will interview you and may place you in administrative processing (221g) if additional security checks are needed.
6. If approved, your new F-1 visa stamp will be placed in your passport and you can re-enter the US.

Important: There is no guarantee of approval at renewal — you must again demonstrate non-immigrant intent (Section 214b) and that you are maintaining valid F-1 status. Students who have been out of status or who have overstayed are typically denied at this stage.

## Automatic 180-Day EAD Extension During STEM OPT Application
This is one of the most important and least-known rules for STEM OPT students, and it prevents thousands of students from an unnecessary work gap every year.

When a student timely files a STEM OPT extension application (Form I-765) before their current standard OPT EAD expires, USCIS automatically grants a 180-day extension of their current work authorization. This means:

- You can legally continue working full-time for your employer without any interruption, even while USCIS is still processing your STEM OPT application.
- The 180-day automatic extension begins the day your standard OPT EAD would have expired and continues until either: (a) USCIS approves or denies your STEM OPT application, or (b) 180 days pass, whichever comes first.
- Your employer does NOT need to re-verify your work authorization during this period. You simply show them your expired EAD plus the I-797C receipt notice from USCIS confirming your pending STEM OPT application as proof of your continued authorization.
- CRITICAL: This 180-day extension is ONLY triggered if you file your STEM OPT application BEFORE your standard OPT EAD expiration date. If you miss the filing deadline and your EAD expires first, you lose the automatic extension and cannot work until USCIS approves your STEM OPT.

## Economic Hardship Off-Campus Work Authorization
F-1 students who experience severe, unforeseen economic hardship may apply for off-campus work authorization specifically due to that hardship — separate from and in addition to CPT and OPT.

Qualifying hardship circumstances include:
- Loss of financial support from your home country due to currency devaluation or economic crisis
- Unexpected loss of a financial sponsor (e.g., death of a parent or sponsor)
- Natural disaster or civil unrest in your home country disrupting funding
- Sudden, unexpected medical expenses for the student or immediate family
- Unexpected change in your financial situation beyond your control

How to apply:
- You must have been in valid F-1 status for at least one full academic year.
- You must obtain a recommendation letter from your DSO confirming the hardship.
- You file Form I-765 with USCIS with the code (c)(3)(iii) and pay the filing fee.
- If approved, you are authorized to work off-campus for up to 20 hours per week during the semester and full-time during breaks.
- Economic hardship authorization is initially granted for one year and can be renewed annually as long as the hardship continues and you maintain good academic standing.
- This authorization counts toward your F-1 status maintenance but does NOT count against or reduce your CPT or OPT eligibility.
